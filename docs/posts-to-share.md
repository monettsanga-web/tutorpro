### Post 1 — speaking at home

> Something we get asked constantly: *"How can I help my child with English
> when my own English isn't strong?"*
>
> The honest answer is that your English level matters far less than you think.
> What matters much more is how often, and whether your child feels safe being
> wrong.
>
> Three things that help more than anything else:
>
> ▪️ **Short and often beats long and rare.** Ten minutes most days does more
> than an hour at the weekend.
>
> ▪️ **Don't correct every mistake.** A child corrected on every sentence stops
> making sentences. If they say "I goed to the park", just reply "Did you? Who
> did you go with?" They hear the right form and the conversation keeps going.
>
> ▪️ **Ask your child to teach you.** Explaining something is the strongest
> practice there is — and children love the role reversal.
>
> We wrote the full guide here, free, no sign-up:
> https://www.tutorpro.site/blog/help-child-speak-english-at-home.html
>
> If your child is at the stage where they understand English but won't speak
> it, that guide is written exactly for you.

### Post 2 — reading

> If your child "struggles with reading", it is worth finding out *which*
> reading problem they have — because the three have completely different fixes
> and the wrong activity wastes months.
>
> Try this: ask your child to read a short passage aloud, then ask what it was
> about.
>
> 📕 **Stumbles on words, guesses at unfamiliar ones** → decoding. They need
> phonics, whatever their age.
>
> 📗 **Reads every word correctly but slowly and flatly** → fluency. Re-read the
> *same* short passage three times. Counter-intuitive, and it works.
>
> 📘 **Reads beautifully but can't tell you what it meant** → comprehension.
> Usually a vocabulary gap.
>
> One thing most of us get wrong: we pick books that are slightly too hard,
> trying to stretch them. For reading practice, easier is better. Save the hard
> book for when you read it *to* them.
>
> Full guide with activities for each:
> https://www.tutorpro.site/blog/english-reading-activities-primary.html

### Post 3 — grammar (the one parents find reassuring)

> When your child says **"I goed to school"** — that is not a mistake going
> backwards. It is a sign of progress.
>
> They have worked out that English past tense adds *-ed*, and they are
> applying the rule to a verb that breaks it. A child who says "went" might
> have just memorised it. A child who says "goed" has understood the system.
>
> The irregular ones come with time.
>
> Genuinely worth practising instead: articles (*a / an / the*), question word
> order ("Why you are late?"), and the third-person *-s* ("he go" → "he goes").
>
> We wrote up the six mistakes we see most, why each happens, and which ones
> are actually worth correcting:
> https://www.tutorpro.site/blog/common-english-grammar-mistakes-children.html

### Post 4 — choosing a school (works well, because it is not about us)

> If you are looking at online English classes for your child, these are the
> questions worth asking **any** school — including us:
>
> ❓ Is every lesson one-to-one, or are some group classes? *(In a group of six,
> your child speaks for maybe a sixth of the lesson.)*
>
> ❓ Did a person actually watch this teacher teach before approving them, or
> did they only check documents?
>
> ❓ Do you get written feedback after every lesson? Ask to see a real example.
> "Great lesson, well done" is worth nothing.
>
> ❓ What is the refund policy, and is it published *before* you pay?
>
> ❓ Is the free trial genuinely free? **A trial that asks for card details is a
> subscription with a delay.**
>
> Full guide, with our own answers to all of them:
> https://www.tutorpro.site/blog/online-english-classes-what-parents-should-know.html

### Version B — replying to a parent who asks for help

Use this when someone asks a question. **Answer first. Link second — or not at
all.**

> That silent stage is really common and it usually isn't stalling — a lot of
> children absorb for weeks before they produce anything.
>
> What helped most with the children I teach: stop correcting for a while.
> Completely. If they say something wrong, just reply naturally using the
> correct form and keep the conversation going. Most children go quiet because
> speaking has started to feel like a test.
>
> Also try giving them a real reason to speak — ordering their own food,
> asking a shop assistant something. One real exchange beats twenty exercises.
>

> that matters most.)

# What to expect

Honestly: a handful of links will not put you on page one next month. What
they do is get you out of the zero-links position, which is the thing
currently holding everything back regardless of how good the pages are.

Realistic: a few links over a few months, plus the pages already built, gives
you a genuine chance at specific searches like *"online English tutor for kids
Philippines"* or *"Cambridge English classes for children"*. Broad competitive
phrases take much longer and depend on how much gets shared.

**One good post in an active parent group is worth more than ten directory
listings.** Directories are permanent but weak; a real parent sharing your
guide is a strong signal and can bring actual bookings, not just rankings.

Anyone promising you first-page rankings on a timeline is selling something.
